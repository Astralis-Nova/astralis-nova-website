🐍 Encounter Log #001

Double Snake Day

Gopher Snake + Baby Rattlesnake

📅 August 30, 2026📍 Cactus Forest / Florence, Arizona⚠️ Venomous encounter included
“Found two snakes in the yard today. One was a gopher snake. The other was a baby rattlesnake that I almost stepped on because I didn't see it. Arizona apparently scheduled a double feature.”

Two snakes turned up around the yard on the same day, and they were a perfect example of why identifying a snake matters. The gopher snake is nonvenomous and useful around property because it hunts rodents. The young rattlesnake is venomous and deserves plenty of space. Small does not mean harmless.

NONVENOMOUS

Gopher Snake

A native constrictor and valuable rodent hunter. Gopher snakes may hiss loudly, flatten the head and rapidly vibrate the tail when threatened. In dry vegetation that tail vibration can sound surprisingly rattlesnake-like.

  • Not venomous.
  • Helps control mice, rats, gophers and other small prey.
  • Can imitate some rattlesnake-style defensive behavior.
  • Best response in most situations: give it room and allow it to move away.
VENOMOUS

Baby Rattlesnake

Young rattlesnakes are venomous from birth. Their small size can make them harder to notice around rocks, plants, fencing and yard clutter.

  • Venomous, even when very small.
  • A bite is a medical emergency.
  • Do not depend on hearing a rattle. Young snakes may have only a small button and rattlesnakes do not always rattle before defending themselves.
  • The common claim that babies are automatically “more dangerous because they cannot control their venom” is a myth. Any rattlesnake bite should be treated seriously.

⚠️ Desert Yard Safety Takeaway

  • Watch where you step, especially near rocks, trees, fencing, pots, brush, lumber and shaded edges.
  • Never use head shape alone to identify a snake. Several harmless species can flatten their heads when defensive.
  • If you cannot identify a snake confidently from a safe distance, treat it cautiously and give it space.
  • Keep children and pets away from an unknown or venomous snake.
  • If a rattlesnake bite occurs, call emergency services or poison control and get medical care promptly. Do not cut the bite, suck out venom, apply ice, or use a tourniquet.

🌵 Field Commentary

Gopher snake: “Just passing through. Rodent-control department.”

Baby rattlesnake: “Tiny body. Extremely serious terms and conditions.”

Human: “Almost stepped on that one. New policy: look down before boldly going anywhere.”

🐍 Encounter Log #002

Two Rattlers, One Release Day

Two Western Diamondbacks • Caught and Released

📅 October 25, 2025📍 Cactus Forest / Florence, Arizona⚠️ Two venomous encounters
“Two rattlesnakes in one day. Both were caught around the property and released away from the house. Apparently the desert had scheduled another double feature before I knew that was going to become a theme.”

These three original clips record two rattlesnake encounters from October 25, 2025: a close look at one snake in the container, a nighttime release, and a daylight release. The strong black-and-white tail bands and dorsal diamond pattern are characteristic of Western diamondback rattlesnakes.

Important safety note: This is a personal field record, not a capture tutorial. Rattlesnakes are venomous, fast at close range, and unpredictable when restrained. Visitors should keep people and pets away and contact an experienced snake remover rather than attempting capture or relocation themselves.

🌵 Field Commentary

First rattler: “Your property inspection is complete.”

Second rattler: “I’m here for the night shift.”

Human: “Two safe releases. Zero invitations to return.”

🦊 Encounter Log #003

Loss, Then a Little Life

Coyote Break-In • A Flock Lost • One New Beginning

📅 October 2025📍 Cactus Forest / Florence, Arizona⚠️ Graphic hatching footage
“It’s been a rough few days. Coyotes broke through what I thought was a secure zone on my property. I lost my duck and five of my chickens—including Trader Joe. That one hits the hardest.”

Trader Joe was special: the only fertilized egg that hatched out of the dozen I bought from Trader Joe’s. I raised him from the moment he cracked that shell. Losing a duck and five chickens was already painful, but losing the bird with that history made the attack hit even harder.

Graphic-content warning: The hatching footage below shows a newborn chick emerging wet from its shell. It is a real birth and may be uncomfortable for some viewers. The videos do not show the coyote killing the birds.

Funny how life works: heartbreaking one day, hopeful the next. Just yesterday, this new baby chick hatched—a reminder that even after loss, life finds a way to start over again.

⚠️ Predator-Safety Takeaway

  • Coyotes can climb, jump, dig and exploit surprisingly small gaps in fencing or gates.
  • After an attack, inspect the entire enclosure—including the roofline, ground edge, corners and latches—because the entry point may not be obvious.
  • Use sturdy hardware cloth or welded wire where practical; ordinary chicken wire is designed to contain poultry, not stop determined predators.
  • Secure birds before dusk and avoid leaving feed or carcasses where they may attract another visit.

🌵 Field Commentary

The desert: “Nothing here is guaranteed.”

The hatchling: “I’m here anyway.”

Human: “Trader Joe won’t be forgotten. We reinforce, we remember, and we keep going.”
