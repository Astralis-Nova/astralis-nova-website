# Rainbow Promise: ark and ancient landscape

Created October 9, 2026 with the built-in image-generation tool.

`ancient-flood-landscape.webp`: photorealistic imagined ancient uplands after the
flood, with rocky eroded slopes, bare tree remains, exposed sediment, receding
slate-blue floodwater, mist and clearing storm clouds. No modern buildings,
roads or landscaped greenery. The clear foreground water is animated separately.

`timber-ark.webp`: transparent reconstruction of a substantial enclosed ancient
timber ark, with long continuous roofing, a ventilation opening below the eaves,
heavy framing, caulked planks, a closed side door and a dark pitch-coated hull.
No sails, mast, modern machinery, glowing windows or decorative lighting.
Transparent borders were trimmed; both assets were converted to WebP for delivery.

The brief was informed by Genesis 6:14–16 (wood, pitch, dimensions, side door,
three decks) and Genesis 8:3–5 (receding water and mountains). The precise date,
geography, wood species, construction and surviving vessel appearance are not
established. These images are artistic interpretations, not documentary evidence
or a claim of a specific archaeological reconstruction.

Reference: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%206%3A14-16%3B8%3A3-5&version=KJV

The renderer displaces only foreground water, clips the lower hull beneath the
waterline, and uses the actual ark image for its broken reflection. Both share a
gentle heave. It uses small displacement ripples rather than a powered wake.
It pauses offscreen and in hidden tabs, follows reduced-motion settings and
cleans up on page exit. Original birds, rainbow and opt-in audio remain intact.

The October 9 refinement gives the water a varying crest across the hull,
independent of the ship's delayed heave and pitch. The submerged hull boundary
follows those crests; reflection samples the same rotated ship rendering.
Contact shadow, short foam curves and gentle displacement ripples help the
vessel sit within the water instead of above a straight horizontal cut.

## October 10: flight and ocean-motion reference pass

Motion references were researched through the original authors' explanations,
published frame sequences and supplemental-video listings. Video playback was
unavailable in the editing environment; the films were not decoded or watched.

- Berg & Biewener, *Wing and body kinematics of takeoff and landing flight in the
  pigeon* (2010): coordinated changes in body, wing and tail attitude. Includes
  high-speed takeoff, landing and mid-flight movies.
  https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing
- Crandell & Tobalske, *Kinematics and aerodynamics of avian upstrokes during slow
  flight* (2015): the outer wing and feather chord change orientation during
  recovery; different species use different upstroke patterns. Frame sequences
  and supplemental movies informed the dove's flexible recovery stroke, without
  applying diamond-dove measurements to the raven.
  https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/218/16/2518/14222/Kinematics-and-aerodynamics-of-avian-upstrokes
- Ocean Exploration Trust, *Beyond the Wow: The Six Types of Ship Motion*:
  vertical heave, bow-to-stern pitch and lateral sway have distinct meanings and
  combine in moving seas. The animation's former `arkRoll` diagnostic is now
  correctly named `arkPitch`.
  https://nautiluslive.org/video/2020/12/09/beyond-wow-six-types-ship-motion

Photographed wings now use 16 connected triangles per side, with continuous
spanwise folding, chord compression and delayed primary motion. Texture-space
clipping prevents the tips being cropped during the stroke. Mirrored wings retain
level shoulder roots; the body leans into cruise and climb. Direction reversals
occur in distant parts of the route, and the breast retains width during turns.
The underlying photographs remain two-dimensional; this is an articulated
illustration, not a full anatomical three-dimensional simulation.

The ocean now combines three crossing wave trains in perspective space. The
same traveling wave field controls surface texture displacement, a low-resolution
normal-lighting overlay, hull occlusion and reflection distortion. The ark's heave
averages waves under its length with a delayed, damped response; pitch responds
separately at the ends. A thin sheet of water washes over the wet hull, with
crest-dependent contact highlights and subdued displacement rings. Motion pauses
without advancing its clock in hidden tabs and offscreen. Reduced motion preserves
a still, internally consistent pose. Wave sizes and timing are artistic choices,
not a calibrated reconstruction of the ancient flood or the vessel's hydrostatics.
