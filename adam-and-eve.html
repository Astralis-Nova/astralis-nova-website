Genesis 1–3 • Faith Story 03

Adam and Eve: Choice in the Garden

Created with dignity and freedom. Confronted by distorted truth. Changed by one choice—and cared for even beyond Eden.

Enter the storyWhat it means
The Biblical account

Humanity, freedom, and a boundary

Genesis first describes humanity—male and female—as made in God’s image and entrusted with caring for the earth. God calls the completed creation “very good.” Genesis 1:26–31

Genesis 2 focuses closely on the man, the garden, and human companionship. God places the man in Eden to cultivate and care for it. He may eat from the garden’s trees except the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God then forms the woman as a corresponding partner, and the two are described as naked without shame. Genesis 2:7–25

The serpent changes the question

The serpent approaches the woman by misstating God’s instruction and then directly contradicting the warning of death. It promises opened eyes and God-like knowledge. The woman sees that the fruit appears good for food, pleasing, and desirable for gaining wisdom. She eats and gives some to her husband, who is with her, and he also eats. Genesis 3:1–6

Was the forbidden fruit an apple?

Genesis never says apple. It simply calls it fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The apple entered later Western artistic tradition. This story and its artwork intentionally use unfamiliar fruit.

Shame, hiding, and blame

Their eyes are opened, but the first results are vulnerability and shame. They sew fig leaves together and hide when they hear God in the garden. Asked what happened, Adam blames the woman—and indirectly God for giving her to him. Eve says the serpent deceived her. Neither begins with ownership of the choice. Genesis 3:7–13

Consequences beyond the moment

The judgment addresses the serpent, the woman, the man, and the ground. Pain, conflict, exhausting labor, and mortality enter the human experience described by the story. Adam names his wife Eve because she will become the mother of all living. Genesis 3:14–20

Care after failure—and exile

Before sending them from Eden, God makes garments of skin and clothes them. They are then barred from the tree of life, and cherubim guard the way back into the garden. The relationship and environment have changed, but the story does not end with immediate abandonment. Genesis 3:21–24

The choice promised elevation. Its immediate results were shame, hiding, accusation, and separation.

This page summarizes Genesis 1–3. It distinguishes the words of Genesis from later artistic traditions and later biblical interpretations.

Text & tradition

What does Genesis actually say?

No apple is namedThe Hebrew text uses the ordinary word for fruit; it does not identify a species.
Adam is presentGenesis 3:6 says Eve gave fruit to her husband “with her.” The scene is not solely Eve acting while Adam is far away.
Genesis calls it the serpentGenesis 3 does not directly use the names Satan or Devil. Revelation 12:9 and 20:2 later connect “the ancient serpent” with the Devil and Satan.
Two special trees appearThe tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil are both named in Genesis 2:9.
Adam means “human”The Hebrew word ’adam is related to ’adamah, “ground,” reinforcing the story’s image of a human formed from earth.
History and interpretationGenesis presents Adam and Eve as humanity’s first couple. Archaeology cannot independently verify the named couple or identify Eden; faith traditions differ on how literally each element should be understood.
Meaning of the story

Freedom makes responsibility possible

Dignity comes before failure

The story begins with people made in God’s image. Their mistake is serious, but it is not the first thing said about human identity.

Temptation distorts truth

The serpent mixes exaggeration, denial, and a partial truth until the forbidden choice appears reasonable.

Desire can narrow vision

The fruit becomes attractive enough that the warning, relationship, and long-term consequence fade from view.

Shame prefers hiding

After the choice, Adam and Eve cover themselves and withdraw instead of moving honestly toward accountability.

Blame delays responsibility

Adam points toward Eve; Eve points toward the serpent. Explanation replaces confession.

Consequences do not erase care

They leave Eden, yet God clothes them first. Judgment and continued concern appear in the same closing movement.

Reflection: When you recognize a wrong choice, do you hide, blame, or move toward honest responsibility—and what would one truthful next step look like?
